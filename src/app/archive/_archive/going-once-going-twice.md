Last week, at a live auction in Miami, world champion auctioneer Wayne Wheat took bids on domain names.

_Democratic.com_, _borrow.com_, _clunky.com_. _Capable.ai_, _girlphotos.com_, _intl.org_. _Fiancee.com_, _autonymous.com_ (note the spellings—I’ll get back to that). There’s also one physical asset up for auction: “The Domain King” Rick Schwartz’s 2010 Bentley.

This auction is a fixture at NamesCon, the who’s-who domain investing conference of the year. It’s the same one that Ingrid Burrington [brilliantly covered](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/02/domain-names-dot-horse/516438/) a decade ago. A few hundred domain investors (slang: “domainers”) and spectators were there, the minority of us holding those paper-sign-on-a-popsicle-stick auction paddles for bidding, and the majority holding cocktails goading their friends to bid (the auction organizer said before the show that one mistake last year was only serving beer and wine, and no cocktails). There’s one guy walking around in an old gray t-shirt that just says “cigarette.com” on the back.

I’ve been to NamesCon twice now. I went once as a fly on the wall—my core memory from last year’s auction was the $3.1 million sale of omegle.com, which any 2010s middle schooler should remember. This year I’m going as a bidder—#327—with my eye on clunky.com.

There are online bidders, too, who Wayne Wheat calls “Internet” throughout the night, as if they’re a single unit. “Do I hear five thousand? Looking at you, Internet.” / “Internet, what do you say?” / “Internet, you’re out.”

Wheat comes up with shorthand for recurring bidders. He calls one of the domainers in the room “greenshirt,” a gentleman in a green polo. (Greenshirt plays small ball, spending a few thousand dollars at most, like the $3,000 he paid for poplars.com.) The fellow controlling the auction computer gets the nickname “internet clerk.”

Wheat also gets visibly frustrated at the actual internet, which cuts out intermittently throughout the night—a big problem for a hybrid offline-online auction. We are at an internet conference for domains and cloud hosting, so there’s a good bit of that “we’re having technological difficulties” irony that you see at hackathons and tech demo nights.

Wayne Wheat is his real name, by the way. I asked him after the auction, because it sounded too good to be true for an auctioneer. He says his friends told him to run for office with a name like that. Ron Jackson wrote a [profile](https://www.dnjournal.com/cover/2016/may-june.htm) about Wheat’s life: paperboy in small Texas town; paratrooper jumping out of airplanes; auctioneer for 30+ years; author of a well-selling book about auctions (which he plugged about halfway through, appropriately I thought). Auctioneering is a specialized vocal skill, like yodeling or rapping or scat, and Wheat has a knack for it (harder than it sounds—try it). He speaks at a normal pace in regular conversation.

## clunky.com

So, I mentioned my interest in clunky.com. Let me back up. In order to learn and write about domain investing, I decided before the conference that I would become a domainer myself. In that room of quiet multimillionaires, I’m a very small fish. And actually, a lot of these domainers are not quiet at all—they’re just not known outside this cottage industry. All in, I plan to spend a few thousand dollars of my own savings flipping domains (it’s research and marketing for the book, right?). Why not start with a splash at the live auction?

When I scanned through the list of domains beforehand, clunky.com was the only one that raised my heart rate, imagining my #327 bidding paddle in the air. For starters, I like the word. _CLUNG-kee_. And I suspected it might be a relative bargain. One domainer I talked to called it a “neg,” as in negative—that is, the word does not have a positive connotation, so it’s not an obvious fit for a brand name, say. (Dumpy.com is another such neg that caught my eye in my very first book newsletter, [Domains by Wordle](/archive/wordle).)

In the end, though, I did not bid for clunky.com, or any other domain. The only time I raised my bidding paddle was during the practice round that Wheat led to kick off the night. (What a thrill.) I was nervous that I’d mindlessly raise it—to scratch my head or something—so I tucked it in my bag after the first few rounds. I didn’t want to blow my whole budget on one name. And that one name, clunky.com—well, I’m not sure if it even sold. It _appeared_ to sell during the auction, for $9,000 to a well-known domainer. But when I checked online, it was marked “Reserve not met.” And now, I can’t find it on the auction website at all.

So I’m not sure who owns clunky.com. If it’s you, reading this, I’d consider paying one thousand dollars. Sorry if that’s a lowball—I don’t mean to offend—I’m just new here is all. One domainer I asked appraised clunky.com at four thousand dollars, another at ten grand, and one of the speakers promoting the auction at as high as $100k, although I’m not entirely sure about their ulterior motives. I met one domainer who was both buying and selling, for example, so it’s possible participants would talk up their own names, or those of their buddies. One domainer I talked to called the industry “incestuous.”

Speaking of, this whole thing—writing about my experience as a domainer—feels like a journalism ethics minefield. I’ll be negotiating in public, so I’ll be careful not to offer any quid pro quo “sell me this domain and I’ll write a flattering piece about it” deal. Whatever I write will be independent, with appropriate disclosure of financial interest.

## Domainers

Financially, most bidders seem uninterested in the domain names on offer this year.

None of the big ticket names—borrow.com, fare.com, democratic.com, capable.ai—met their reserve prices. I don’t think there was a single six-figure sale, let alone a multimillion-dollar deal like omegle.com last year. But my favorite part about covering domains is the wordplay, and the wordplay is just as fun when thrifting. Most domainers, I think, would say their favorite part is the money, but many of them also have a soft spot for the poetics of the trade, I gather.

“Does it pass the radio test?”

That’s one of the first questions you ask when valuing a name. That is, if I say a domain name aloud, would you spell it out correctly on paper? Maybe that’s why fare.com didn’t sell. My own book domain, dotcom.press, fails the radio test spectacularly—whoops.

Then again, we can’t all spell very well. I learned, for example, that fiancée with two _e_’s describes a woman engaged to be married, whereas fiancé with just one _e_ denotes a man (I’m currently a fiancé myself). Bids for fiancee.com—the female spelling—topped out at $18,000, below its reserve price (meaning it did not sell, although the online auction is still going). I talked to that seller, who earlier in the night won offences.com for $1,750. I asked him about going for the British spelling rather than the American offenses-with-an-_s_, but he hadn’t realized. He shot me a left-my-keys-in-the-car sort of look. “You’re scaring me,” he said. I reflexively tried to reassure him that, hey, what do I know. I didn’t even know about the second _e_ in fiancée. Did _he_ know about the second _e_? (I followed up with him after, and yes, he did know about the second _e_, thinking the female spelling would be much more valuable.)

Autonymous.com is a misspelling of “autonomous,” as in autonomous vehicles. The auction promoters were keen to point out that autonymous-with-a-_y_ is also a dictionary word. You _can_ find it in some online dictionaries—merriam-webster.com defines it as “naming or designating itself”—but it’s not _really_ a word in that it’s never been meaningfully used. According to Google’s Ngram Viewer, “autonymous” has appeared in, at best, 0.0000002% of books over the last ~200 years. And as I’m writing now, my word processor underlines autonymous with red squigglies. That doesn’t mean it’s not a valuable domain, though. Through the frowned-upon practice of [typosquatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typosquatting), a domainer could make money from typos. Autonymous.com sold for $1,000 (per my notes, although I have not seen it publicly reported yet).

Late in the auction, I meet a domainer named David, an Italian-American from the Bronx who makes the best biscotti and whose ma makes the best meatballs and tomatuh sauce (his words). Still riding the high from a $3,000 domain purchase—not yet paid for, so he didn’t want me to include the name—he starts telling me old domainer stories, like the time he got within earshot of Robert De Niro and yelled, “Hey Bobby! You wanna buy abronxtale.com?” (De Niro had starred in the movie A Bronx Tale. David still owns the domain.) While we’re talking, he can’t turn his domain brain off. He keeps stopping mid-sentence, turning to reflexively appraise the names for sale: restorememory.com, microbeer.com, cheapgas.com. Which—I get it. This domaining thing is quite fun. I’m having fun, we’re having fun. David is someone I’d like to spend more time around.

## Doing my bidding

And I will spend more time around David—and all these other domainers. Or at least I’ll be showing up in the spaces where they hang out: expired domain auctions (called [drop catching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_drop_catching) in the biz), domain sales statistics sites, domainer podcasts, these conferences.

The truth is, I have a lot of dissonance about domain investing, even a distaste. It bums me out that all these pithy turns of phrase are parked by investors rather than enjoyed by individuals and businesses, for websites and email addresses. But can I blame them? Can you blame real estate investors for owning houses on Central Park they never live in? Or art collectors whose masterpieces sit in a Swiss warehouse for safekeeping?

Don’t knock it ’til you try it, I guess.
